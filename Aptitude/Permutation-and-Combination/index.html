1. Introduction & Why This Topic Matters

Permutation & Combination (P&C) is a foundational topic that appears in every competitive exam — GATE (Discrete Maths / Aptitude), ESE, PSU recruitment tests, and Bank exams (Quantitative Aptitude). Mastering it unlocks Probability, Counting, and Combinatorics questions, which together can contribute 3–8 marks.

Analogy — The Two Core Questions Think of P&C as answering exactly two flavors of question:
Permutation = “In how many ways can I arrange things?” (Order matters — like seats in a row.)
Combination = “In how many ways can I select things?” (Order does NOT matter — like picking a team.)

2. Fundamental Counting Principle

Multiplication Rule (AND)

If task A can be done in m ways and then task B can be done in n ways, the total ways to do A and B = m × n.

Analogy You have 3 shirts and 4 pants. Total outfits = 3 × 4 = 12. Each shirt pairs with every pant — an independent sequential choice.
Generalized Multiplication Rule If a task consists of k sequential stages with n₁, n₂, …, nₖ options respectively, total ways = n₁ × n₂ × … × nₖ

Addition Rule (OR)

If task A can be done in m ways or task B in n ways, and they are mutually exclusive (cannot happen simultaneously), the total ways = m + n.

Example Q: A student can travel to college by bus (5 routes) or by train (3 routes). Total ways?
A: 5 + 3 = 8 (the student takes one or the other, not both simultaneously).
Trick — AND vs OR Detector • See “and” / “then” / sequential stages → Multiply
• See “or” / “either” / mutually exclusive choices → Add
Edge Case If choices are not mutually exclusive, use Inclusion–Exclusion: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. Forgetting this is a classic exam trap.
GATE-Style Example Q: How many 3-digit numbers can be formed from digits {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} with no repetition?
A: Hundreds place: 5 choices → Tens: 4 remaining → Units: 3 remaining.
Total = 5 × 4 × 3 = 60.

3. Factorial — Concept, Properties & Derivation

What Is a Factorial?

n! (read “n factorial”) = the product of all positive integers from 1 to n.

Definition n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … × 2 × 1
Special cases: 0! = 1 (by convention / empty product),   1! = 1

Why Does n! Exist?

Analogy — Arranging Books Place 4 distinct books on a shelf.
• 1st slot: 4 choices • 2nd: 3 • 3rd: 2 • 4th: 1
Total = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 4! = 24.
The factorial naturally emerges when you arrange all n distinct objects in a line.

Why 0! = 1?

The recurrence n! = n × (n−1)! gives 1! = 1 × 0!, so 0! must be 1. Alternatively, there is exactly one way to arrange zero objects: do nothing.

Key Properties

  • n! = n × (n−1)!  —  recursive definition
  • n! / (n−r)! = n × (n−1) × … × (n−r+1)  —  exactly r falling terms
  • n! grows super-exponentially: 10! = 3,628,800;   20! ≈ 2.43 × 1018

Example: 100! has ⌊100/5⌋ + ⌊100/25⌋ = 20 + 4 = 24 trailing zeros.

4. Permutation Formula — Derivation & Intuition

The Core Question

“In how many ways can we arrange r objects out of n distinct objects?”

Step-by-Step Derivation

Imagine filling r slots from n distinct items (no repetition):

  • Slot 1: n choices
  • Slot 2: n−1 choices
  • Slot 3: n−2 choices
  • …
  • Slot r: n−r+1 choices

By the Multiplication Rule:

Permutation Formula P(n, r) = ⁿPᵣ = n × (n−1) × … × (n−r+1) = n! / (n−r)!

Why divide by (n−r)!?
The product n × (n−1) × … × 1 = n! goes all the way down to 1, but we only need r terms. Dividing by (n−r)! chops off the unwanted tail: (n−r) × (n−r−1) × … × 1.

Special Cases

  • P(n, n) = n! / 0! = n! — arrange all n objects
  • P(n, 0) = n! / n! = 1 — select & arrange nothing (one way: do nothing)
  • P(n, 1) = n — just picking one item
Example — Filling Positions Q: 10 athletes compete. How many ways to award Gold, Silver, Bronze?
A: P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.
Order matters: (A=Gold, B=Silver) ≠ (B=Gold, A=Silver).
Aha Moment Permutation = Selection + Arrangement. First pick r items, then order them. That ordering step is what distinguishes permutation from combination.

5. Permutation — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Word Arrangements Q: How many 4-letter words (meaningful or not) from the letters of “GATE”?
A: All 4 letters are distinct. Arrange all 4: P(4, 4) = 4! = 24.
Example 2 — Digit Constraints Q: How many 4-digit even numbers can be formed from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} without repetition?
A: The units digit must be even: {2, 4} → 2 choices.
Remaining 3 digits from 4 left: P(4, 3) = 24.
Total = 2 × 24 = 48.
Trick: Always fix the constrained position first.
Example 3 — Keeping Together Q: Arrange A, B, C, D, E such that A and B are always together.
A: Treat {AB} as a single block → 4 entities: {AB}, C, D, E → 4! arrangements.
Within the block, A & B can swap: 2!.
Total = 4! × 2! = 24 × 2 = 48.
Example 4 — Never Together Q: Arrange A, B, C, D, E such that A and B are never adjacent.
A: Total arrangements = 5! = 120.
Together = 48 (from above).
Never together = 120 − 48 = 72.
Trick: “Never together” = Total − Together. Always use the complement.
Trick — Constrained Permutation Strategy 1. Fix the most restricted position first (even digit, specific person, etc.).
2. Use block/bundle method for “together” constraints.
3. Use complement (Total − unwanted) for “never” constraints.
4. Use gap method for “no two X adjacent” problems: arrange others first, then place X in gaps.

6. Combination Formula — Derivation & Intuition

The Core Question

“In how many ways can we select r objects from n distinct objects, without caring about order?”

Derivation from Permutation

The Key Insight Every combination of r items can be internally rearranged in r! ways to produce permutations.
So: P(n, r) = C(n, r) × r!

Solving: C(n, r) = P(n, r) / r! = n! / [r! × (n−r)!]
Aha Moment — Why Divide by r!? Suppose you picked 3 people {A, B, C} for a committee. Permutation counts ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA as 6 different outcomes. But as a committee, all 6 are the same group. Dividing by 3! = 6 collapses them into one.

Properties of C(n, r)

  • Symmetry: C(n, r) = C(n, n−r) — choosing r to include = choosing (n−r) to exclude
  • Pascal’s Identity: C(n, r) = C(n−1, r−1) + C(n−1, r)
  • C(n, 0) = C(n, n) = 1
  • C(n, 1) = n
  • Sum of row: C(n,0) + C(n,1) + … + C(n,n) = 2ⁿ (total subsets)

Pascal’s Triangle (Visualize C(n,r))

          1
        1   1
      1   2   1
    1   3   3   1
  1   4   6   4   1
1   5  10  10   5   1

Each entry = sum of the two directly above it. Row n gives C(n, 0), C(n, 1), …, C(n, n).

7. Combination — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Committee Selection Q: From 8 people, choose a committee of 3. How many ways?
A: C(8, 3) = 8! / (3! × 5!) = (8 × 7 × 6) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 56.
Example 2 — Handshake Problem Q: 12 people in a room. Each shakes hands with every other exactly once. Total handshakes?
A: A handshake = choosing 2 people (order irrelevant). C(12, 2) = (12 × 11) / 2 = 66.
Example 3 — With Constraints Q: A committee of 5 from 6 men and 4 women, with at least 2 women.
A: Cases: (2W, 3M), (3W, 2M), (4W, 1M).
= C(4,2)×C(6,3) + C(4,3)×C(6,2) + C(4,4)×C(6,1)
= 6×20 + 4×15 + 1×6 = 120 + 60 + 6 = 186.
Alternative (complement): Total − (0W + 1W) = C(10,5) − C(6,5) − C(4,1)×C(6,4) = 252 − 6 − 60 = 186. ✓
Trick — Quick C(n,r) Calculation For small r, use the “falling product” shortcut:
C(n, r) = [n × (n−1) × … × (n−r+1)] / r!
Example: C(20, 3) = (20 × 19 × 18) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 6840 / 6 = 1140.
Never expand full factorials in exams — cancel early!

8. Permutation vs Combination — Decision Framework

The One Question That Decides Everything

“Does rearranging the selected items give a DIFFERENT outcome?”

  • Yes → Permutation (order matters)
  • No → Combination (order doesn’t matter)

Comparison Table

AspectPermutationCombination
KeywordArrange, Order, Rank, Position, SequenceSelect, Choose, Pick, Group, Committee, Team
Formulan! / (n−r)!n! / [r!(n−r)!]
RelationshipP = C × r!C = P / r!
AlwaysP(n,r) ≥ C(n,r)C(n,r) ≤ P(n,r)
ExamplePIN code 1234 ≠ 4321Lottery draw {1,2,3} = {3,1,2}
Quick Classifier Examples 1. “Forming a password of 4 digits” → Permutation (1234 ≠ 4321).
2. “Choosing 3 toppings for pizza” → Combination (toppings have no order).
3. “Electing president, VP, secretary from 10” → Permutation (positions are distinct).
4. “Selecting 4 cards from 52” → Combination (just selection, no arrangement).
5. “Seating 5 guests in 5 chairs” → Permutation (each chair is a distinct slot).
Common Exam Trap “How many ways to choose 3 books from 10 and arrange them on a shelf?”
This is NOT a pure combination! It’s selection + arrangement = P(10, 3) = 720.
Read the complete question — the last phrase decides the answer.

9. Permutations with Repetition Allowed

When Each Choice Is Independent

If repetition is allowed and you fill r positions from n choices, each slot has n options independently.

Formula — Permutation with Repetition Total ways = nr
(Each of the r positions has n independent choices.)
Example 1 — License Plates Q: A license plate has 3 letters (A–Z) followed by 3 digits (0–9). How many plates?
A: Letters: 263. Digits: 103. Total = 263 × 103 = 17,576 × 1,000 = 17,576,000.
Example 2 — Binary Strings Q: How many binary strings of length n?
A: Each bit has 2 choices → 2n. For n = 8: 256.
Example 3 — GATE Style Q: How many 4-digit numbers can be formed using {0,1,2,3,4,5} with repetition?
A: Thousands place: 5 choices (1–5, cannot be 0).
Remaining 3 places: 6 choices each.
Total = 5 × 63 = 5 × 216 = 1080.
Trap: Don’t write 64 = 1296. The leading digit cannot be 0!
Trick — Quick Decision: With or Without Repetition? • “Repetition allowed” / “digits can repeat” → nr
• “Distinct” / “no repetition” / “without replacement” → P(n, r) = n! / (n−r)!
If the problem says nothing, default to without repetition in most exam contexts.

10. Arrangements of Objects with Identical Items (Multinomial)

The Problem

How many distinct arrangements of n objects where some are identical?

Multinomial Permutation Formula If there are n objects with p₁ identical of type 1, p₂ identical of type 2, …, pₖ identical of type k (where p₁ + p₂ + … + pₖ = n):

Distinct arrangements = n! / (p₁! × p₂! × … × pₖ!)

This is called the multinomial coefficient because it generalizes the binomial coefficient C(n, r) = n!/(r!(n−r)!) from 2 groups to k groups.

Why Divide?

Aha Moment Consider “AAB”. If you label the As as A₁, A₂, you get 3! = 6 arrangements. But A₁A₂B and A₂A₁B look the same. Each arrangement has been over-counted by 2! (ways to swap As). So divide: 3! / 2! = 3 (AAB, ABA, BAA).
Example 1 — Classic Word Problem Q: Arrangements of “MISSISSIPPI”?
A: 11 letters: M(1), I(4), S(4), P(2).
= 11! / (1! × 4! × 4! × 2!) = 39916800 / (1 × 24 × 24 × 2) = 39916800 / 1152 = 34,650.
Example 2 — Grid Paths Q: Shortest paths in a grid from (0,0) to (m,n) moving only right (R) or up (U)?
A: Total steps = m + n (m Rights and n Ups). We arrange m R’s and n U’s:
= (m+n)! / (m! × n!) = C(m+n, m).
This beautifully connects grid paths to combinations!
Exam Shortcut For any word/string problem, just list letter frequencies, write n! on top, and divide by each frequency factorial. No need to think deeper.

11. Combinations with Repetition (Stars & Bars)

The Problem

“Choose r items from n types when repetition is allowed and order doesn’t matter.”

Analogy — Ice Cream Scoops You get 4 scoops from 3 flavors (Vanilla, Chocolate, Strawberry). You can repeat flavors. How many distinct combos?
This is NOT C(3,4) or 34. It’s a “stars and bars” problem!

Stars & Bars Technique

Represent the r items as stars (*) and use (n−1) bars (|) to divide them into n groups (one per type).

Visual Example 4 scoops, 3 flavors: arrange 4 stars and 2 bars.
**|*|* → 2V, 1C, 1S   |   ****|| → 4V, 0C, 0S   |   |**|** → 0V, 2C, 2S
Total arrangements = choosing positions for bars (or stars) from (r + n − 1) slots.
Stars & Bars Formula Ways to choose r items from n types with repetition = C(n + r − 1, r) = C(n + r − 1, n − 1)

For the ice cream example: C(3 + 4 − 1, 4) = C(6, 4) = 15.
GATE-Style — Non-Negative Integer Solutions Q: Number of non-negative integer solutions of x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = 10?
A: Distributing 10 identical units into 3 variables = stars & bars.
= C(10 + 3 − 1, 10) = C(12, 10) = C(12, 2) = 66.
Edge Case — Positive Integer Solutions If x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = 10 with each xᵢ ≥ 1 (positive), substitute yᵢ = xᵢ − 1:
y₁ + y₂ + y₃ = 7, yᵢ ≥ 0 → C(7 + 2, 2) = C(9, 2) = 36.

12. Circular Permutations

Why Is It Different?

In a circle, there is no fixed starting point. Rotating all persons by one seat gives the same relative arrangement.

Aha Moment Seating {A, B, C, D} in a line: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC are 4 different arrangements.
In a circle: they are all the same (same neighbors). So circular arrangements = linear / n.
Circular Permutation Formula Arrange n distinct objects in a circle = (n − 1)!

Derivation: Linear arrangements = n!. Each circular arrangement maps to n linear ones (n rotations). So circular = n! / n = (n−1)!.
Example 1 Q: 6 people sit around a round table. How many ways?
A: (6 − 1)! = 5! = 120.
Example 2 — Fixed Reference Q: 6 people around a table, but the host sits in a specific chair. How many ways?
A: Fixing the host breaks circular symmetry → remaining 5 people: 5! = 120.
Note: Same answer! Fixing one person in circular = (n−1)! by definition.
Example 3 — Relative Constraints Q: 8 people around a table, 2 specific people must sit together.
A: Treat pair as a block → 7 entities in a circle: (7−1)! = 6! = 720.
Internal arrangement of the pair: 2!.
Total = 720 × 2 = 1440.
Edge Case — Numbered Chairs If the circular table has numbered/labeled seats, it becomes a linear problem! Use n!, not (n−1)!.
Exam trap: Read whether seats are distinguishable.

13. Necklace & Bracelet Problems

Necklace (Clockwise-Only)

Same as circular permutation: (n − 1)!
(Rotations are identical, but flipping gives a different arrangement.)

Bracelet / Keychain (Flipping Allowed)

A bracelet can be flipped over, so clockwise and anticlockwise views are the same.

Bracelet Formula Distinct arrangements = (n − 1)! / 2

Derivation: Each bracelet arrangement corresponds to 2 necklace arrangements (one is the mirror). So divide by 2.
Example — Bead Problem Q: 7 distinct beads on a bracelet. How many arrangements?
A: (7 − 1)! / 2 = 720 / 2 = 360.
Example — Selecting & Arranging on Bracelet Q: Choose 5 beads from 10 distinct beads and arrange on a bracelet.
A: Select: C(10, 5) = 252.
Arrange on bracelet: (5−1)! / 2 = 12.
Total = 252 × 12 = 3024.
Decision Guide • “Round table” / “circular” / “necklace” (one-sided) → (n−1)!
• “Bracelet” / “keychain” / “garland” (can flip) → (n−1)! / 2
• “Numbered seats around table” → n! (linear!)

14. Derangements (Subfactorials)

What Is a Derangement?

A derangement is a permutation where no element appears in its original position.

Analogy — Hat Problem n people put hats in a pile. They randomly pick hats. A derangement = nobody gets their own hat.
Derangement Formula D(n) = n! × [1 − 1/1! + 1/2! − 1/3! + … + (−1)ⁿ/n!]

Equivalently: D(n) = n! × ∑ₖ₌₀ⁿ (−1)ᵏ / k!

Recurrence: D(n) = (n−1) × [D(n−1) + D(n−2)], with D(1) = 0, D(2) = 1.

GATE Example Q: 4 letters and 4 envelopes. How many ways so that NO letter goes into the correct envelope?
A: D(4) = 4! × (1 − 1 + 1/2 − 1/6 + 1/24) = 24 × (1/2 − 1/6 + 1/24) = 24 × (12/24 − 4/24 + 1/24) = 24 × 9/24 = 9.
Quick Computation Trick D(n) = nearest integer to n!/e. Works for n ≥ 1!
Example: D(5) = round(120 / 2.718) = round(44.14) = 44. ✓

15. Inclusion–Exclusion Principle in Counting

The Core Idea

To count elements in the union of sets, add individual sets, subtract pairwise intersections, add triple intersections, and so on.

Inclusion–Exclusion Formula |A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ| = ∑|Aᵢ| − ∑|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| + ∑|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ| − … + (−1)ⁿ⁺¹|A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ|
Example — Divisibility Count Q: How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2, 3, or 5?
A: Let A = div by 2, B = div by 3, C = div by 5.
|A| = 50, |B| = 33, |C| = 20.
|A∩B| = div by 6 = 16, |A∩C| = div by 10 = 10, |B∩C| = div by 15 = 6.
|A∩B∩C| = div by 30 = 3.
|A∪B∪C| = 50+33+20 − 16−10−6 + 3 = 74.
Application — Surjective Functions Q: Number of onto functions from set A (m elements) to set B (n elements)?
A: Using inclusion–exclusion:
Onto(m,n) = ∑ₖ₌₀ⁿ (−1)ᵏ C(n,k) (n−k)ᵐ
This is a GATE favorite. For m=4, n=3: 34 − C(3,1)×24 + C(3,2)×14 = 81 − 48 + 3 = 36.
When to Use Inclusion–Exclusion • “At least one” / “none of” problems — use complement + IE.
• Derangement formula itself is derived from IE!
• Counting surjections, Euler’s totient — all IE applications.

17. Partitions, Committees & Group Formation

Dividing Into Groups of Equal Size

Formula Divide n people into k groups of size m (where n = k × m):
• If groups are labeled (Group A, B, …): n! / (m!)k
• If groups are unlabeled (just “groups”): n! / [(m!)k × k!]
(Divide by k! because swapping entire groups doesn’t create a new partition.)
Example 1 Q: Divide 12 people into 3 groups of 4 for projects (groups unnamed).
A: 12! / [(4!)3 × 3!] = 479001600 / [13824 × 6] = 479001600 / 82944 = 5775.

Committee With a Chair

Example 2 Q: Form a committee of 5 from 12 people and select a chairperson.
A: Method 1: Choose committee C(12,5), then chair C(5,1) = 792 × 5 = 3960.
Method 2: Choose chair C(12,1), then remaining 4 from 11: C(11,4) = 12 × 330 = 3960. ✓

Groups of Unequal Size

Example 3 Q: Divide 10 people into groups of 3, 3, and 4.
A: Groups of 3 are same size (unlabeled): 10! / (3! × 3! × 4! × 2!) = 3628800 / (6 × 6 × 24 × 2) = 3628800 / 1728 = 2100.
Note: divide by 2! only for the two groups of equal size, not for the group of 4.
Common Mistake Students forget to divide by k! for identical groups. If the problem says “distribute to teams A, B, C” (labeled), do NOT divide by k!. If it says “divide into groups,” you must.

18. Exam-Specific Tricks & Speed Techniques

Trick 1: Complement Method

For “at least one,” “never,” or “no two adjacent” — compute Total − Unwanted. Usually faster.

Trick 2: Gap Method (No Two Adjacent)

Example Q: Arrange 5 boys and 3 girls such that no two girls are adjacent.
A: Arrange boys: 5!. Gaps = 6 (_B_B_B_B_B_). Choose 3 gaps for girls: C(6,3). Arrange girls: 3!.
= 5! × C(6,3) × 3! = 120 × 20 × 6 = 14,400.

Trick 3: Fix & Count

For relative-position constraints (A must be left of B), total valid = total arrangements / (number of relative orderings).
If A must be left of B: Valid = n! / 2 (exactly half have A left of B).

Trick 4: Rank of a Word

Example Q: Find the rank of “GATE” in dictionary order of its letters {A, E, G, T}.
A: Words starting with A: 3! = 6. Words starting with E: 3! = 6.
Words starting with G: first is GA…
GAE T = “GAET” is before GATE. Count: GAE_ → 1 word (GAET).
GAT E = “GATE” itself. Rank = 6 + 6 + 1 + 1 = 14.

Trick 5: Use Symmetry in C(n,r)

C(n, r) = C(n, n−r). Always compute the smaller side.
C(100, 98) = C(100, 2) = 4950 — instant vs. impossible by expansion.

Trick 6: Selection with “At Least”

Ways to select at least 1 from n items = 2ⁿ − 1 (total subsets minus empty set).

Speed Formula Sheet • Words from n distinct letters = n!
• Words from n letters with repeats = n! / (freq factorials)
• Choosing r from n = C(n,r)
• At least 1 selection = 2ⁿ − 1
• Diagonal of n-gon = C(n,2) − n = n(n−3)/2
• Onto functions = ∑(−1)ᵏ C(n,k)(n−k)ᵐ

Critical Edge Cases (Exam Traps)

Trap 1 — 0 in Number Formation When forming n-digit numbers, the leading digit cannot be 0. Always handle the first position separately.
Trap 2 — “At Least” vs “Exactly” “At least 2 women” ≠ “Exactly 2 women.” Use complement or case-by-case.
Trap 3 — Labeled vs Unlabeled “Divide into groups” (unlabeled) vs “distribute to persons/boxes” (labeled) changes the answer by k!.
Trap 4 — Circular vs Linear Always check if the arrangement is circular. Using n! instead of (n−1)! is a guaranteed wrong answer.
Trap 5 — With vs Without Repetition Default assumption in most exams: without repetition. But read carefully!
NAT Answer Precision For GATE NAT questions, P&C answers are always exact integers. If you get a decimal, you have made an error. Recheck!

5-Second Decision Flowchart

  1. Ordered? Yes → Permutation. No → Combination.
  2. Repetition? Allowed → nr or Stars & Bars. Not allowed → Standard P/C.
  3. Circular? Yes → (n−1)!. Flippable? → /2.
  4. Identical items? Yes → Multinomial. Check grouping.
  5. Constraints? Use complement, gap, or block method.

Exam Day Checklist

  • Read the question twice. Identify order, repetition, and constraints.
  • Use complement for “at least” and “never” problems.
  • Fix constrained positions first.
  • Cancel factorials early — never expand large ones.
  • Your answer must be a positive integer. If not, recheck.
  • For NAT: double-check arithmetic. For MCQ: plug small values to verify.
